How Hot Does a Sauna Get?

Traditional and infrared saunas operate at very different air temperatures because they create heat differently. Comparing the thermostat numbers without understanding the heating method can be misleading.

Typical temperature ranges

Same goal. Very different numbers.

158–194°F

Traditional sauna

Traditional sauna heaters warm the room air and sauna stones. The hotter air is a major part of the classic sauna experience, and water can be added to the stones for a temporary wave of steam.

113–140°F

Infrared sauna

Infrared emitters radiate heat toward your body, so the cabin does not need the same high air temperature to create a warm sauna experience.

Why we use Fahrenheit: these are the numbers that are meaningful to most Pittsburgh-area customers. Manufacturer materials may also show Celsius, but we’ll keep the Learning Center focused on Fahrenheit.
Temperature is not the whole story

What 130°F feels like depends on how the heat reaches you.

An infrared sauna at 130°F should not be judged as a traditional sauna that “failed to get hot enough.” It is a different heating system. Likewise, someone who loves the enveloping heat and steam ritual of a traditional sauna may find infrared too gentle.

Heat method

Hot room air and stones versus radiant emitters.

Humidity

Traditional allows water on stones; infrared remains dry.

Personal preference

The best temperature is the one that creates an experience you enjoy and use consistently.

Our showroom advice

Do not buy a sauna by chasing the highest temperature.

Choose the heat style first. If you want intense hot air, stones and steam, traditional is the natural place to start. If you prefer radiant warmth at a lower room temperature and quicker startup, start with infrared.

The easiest way to understand the difference: experience both styles. Five minutes sitting in each can tell you more than another hour comparing temperature specifications online.
